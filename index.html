A PRACTICAL SHORT BOOK

My Experience and Advice for Becoming a Professional Customer Service Specialist

A practical short book for customer service specialists and Team Leads.

WHAT THIS BOOK IS ABOUT

Practical lessons from real customer service experience — listening, understanding, empathy, knowledge, confidence, consistency, accountability and continuous improvement.

This book is based on my own work experience in customer service and reflects my personal professional opinion, the lessons I have learned, and the knowledge and expertise I have developed through my experience in the role. The ideas and advice I share come from situations I have experienced, observations I have made, mistakes I have learned from, and approaches that I believe can help someone become a stronger customer service professional.

INTRODUCTION

This book is based on my own work experience in customer service and reflects my personal professional opinion, the lessons I have learned, and the knowledge and expertise I have developed through my experience in the role. The ideas and advice I share throughout this book come from situations I have experienced, observations I have made, mistakes I have learned from, and approaches that I believe can help someone become a stronger customer service professional. I am not presenting these ideas as the only way to work in customer service. Every company, customer, and working environment can be different.

My purpose is to share what I have learned from my own experience and expertise in a practical way that may help others develop in their own customer service careers. Customer service is not just about answering questions. It is a professional responsibility that requires us to understand the customer's inquiry, provide the best possible solution, communicate with empathy, and handle every interaction professionally. At the same time, we need to work within the company's policies, procedures, and safety requirements, making sure that the solution we provide is helpful, accurate, appropriate, and safe for both the customer and the company.

As customer service specialists, our role is to create an experience that gives customers confidence in the support they receive and, ultimately, in the company itself. Even when we cannot provide exactly what a customer is asking for, the way we listen, explain the situation, offer available alternatives, and guide them toward the next step can make a significant difference. Good customer service is therefore not only about finding an answer; it is about finding the right solution and delivering it in the right way.

From my experience, becoming a professional customer service specialist requires a combination of listening, understanding, communication, empathy, knowledge, confidence, efficiency, consistency, accountability, and continuous improvement. These are the principles I believe can turn a simple customer interaction into professional customer support.

CHAPTER 1

Becoming a Professional Customer Service Specialist

1. Listen Before You Respond

Listening is one of the foundations of good customer service because before we can provide a solution, we first need to understand why the customer contacted us. Sometimes the first question a customer asks is only part of the actual problem, so by paying attention to what they are saying, the information they provide, and what they are trying to achieve, we can identify the root of the issue and guide them toward the correct solution.

2. Paraphrase the Customer's Request

Paraphrasing means taking what the customer has told us and restating it in our own words to confirm that we have understood them correctly. Before looking for a solution, we need to make sure that we are solving the correct problem. If we misunderstand the customer's issue and provide a solution for something they did not ask about, we create additional work for ourselves and frustration for the customer. We may then have to go back and start again, negatively affecting AHT and other KPIs such as CSAT, FCR, and quality because the customer may feel that they were not properly heard.

Instead of immediately moving toward a solution, we can confirm our understanding with wording such as, "So, if I understand correctly, you're contacting us because..." This gives the customer an opportunity to confirm our understanding or correct us before we move in the wrong direction. It also demonstrates that we have paid attention to what they told us. Paraphrasing can therefore improve both the customer experience and our efficiency. When the customer and the agent have the same understanding of the problem, it becomes much easier to guide the conversation toward the correct resolution.

3. Show Genuine Empathy

Genuine empathy starts with listening to and understanding the customer, understanding what happened, and acknowledging the situation in a way that is relevant to what they have actually experienced.

Listen → Understand → Paraphrase + Empathy.

4. Know the Company, Policies, Products and Services

Strong knowledge of the company is essential for providing excellent customer service. This includes understanding company policies, products, services, procedures, systems, and the different options available to customers. The stronger our knowledge becomes, the more freedom and confidence we have to go above and beyond for the customer without crossing the boundaries of company policy. Providing a technically correct answer does not always mean that the customer has received a good experience.

Simply telling a customer where they need to go or what form they need to complete may technically answer their question, but a stronger approach is to explain why that is the correct process, what they should expect, what information they may need, and what their next steps will be. This becomes especially important when the customer needs to complete something through self-service after the conversation has ended.

We should therefore think beyond the current chat or call and ask ourselves, "What information will this customer need when they start the next step after our conversation ends?" Providing that information proactively can prevent confusion and reduce unnecessary repeat contact. Strong knowledge also improves efficiency because when we understand the correct solution, we can confidently guide the conversation instead of spending unnecessary time searching for answers. Knowledge gives us control of the conversation and the freedom to go above and beyond without crossing the policy line.

5. Control the Conversation

A simple customer question can sometimes become a very long interaction because the same concern is being asked in several different ways. Controlling the conversation does not mean rushing the customer, interrupting them, or ignoring their concerns. It means listening carefully, identifying the actual issue, confirming our understanding, providing the answer clearly, addressing relevant follow-up concerns, and confidently guiding the interaction toward a resolution. Earlier in my customer-service experience, I had an interaction where I provided the correct answer relatively early in the conversation.

However, because I was still developing my knowledge since I was new and was unsure whether the customers were asking for something else, I allowed the conversation to continue much longer than necessary. I kept showing empathy and doing my job in a professional way. The customers were appreciative of the support, so it was still a positive interaction, but it taught me an important lesson. Confidence comes partly from experience and partly from knowledge.

When we understand the issue and know the answer, we should be confident enough to guide the customer toward the resolution instead of allowing the conversation to repeatedly move in different directions. When you know the answer, lead the conversation with confidence.

6. Understand That You Represent the Company

When we communicate with a customer, we represent the company. Customers may not separate the employee from the organization; to them, the person helping them at that moment is the company. The way we communicate, explain a limitation, handle frustration, or provide a solution can therefore influence how the customer remembers the entire organization. There may be situations where we personally disagree with a policy or completely understand why the customer is frustrated with a particular process.

However, speaking negatively about the company, blaming another department, or simply saying, "That's the company policy, so there is nothing I can do," usually does not improve the situation. Instead, we can acknowledge the customer's frustration, clearly explain the limitation, and move the conversation toward what we can do.

Representing the company professionally does not mean blindly defending every policy; it means communicating professionally, taking responsibility for the interaction, and focusing on the options available to the customer. Every interaction contributes to how that person remembers the company.

7. Consistency

Being a strong customer service specialist is not about having one excellent shift, one excellent week, or one period where all of our KPIs look good. The bigger question is how consistently we can maintain our performance over time. Customer-service KPIs can include customer satisfaction, quality, response or handling time, resolution, and productivity. Different companies may measure performance differently, but consistency remains one of the most important elements behind sustainable results. From the first customer of the day to the last, we should try to maintain the same standard of accuracy and attention.

As we gain experience, the job often becomes more manageable because our knowledge, confidence, and familiarity with different situations increase. The challenge is maintaining that professional standard across every chat, call, and email, for every shift, week, month, quarter, and even for years.

8. Accountability

Accountability means taking responsibility for our own performance and improvement instead of always waiting for a manager, Team Lead, trainer, or quality team to identify our weaknesses for us. One of the most valuable habits we can develop is reviewing our own interactions, especially when something did not go as expected. For example, if we receive a poor customer-satisfaction rating, we should not wait until someone else brings it to our attention.

When possible, we can review the interaction ourselves and ask whether we understood the customer's concern correctly, answered clearly, showed appropriate empathy, provided useful information, and controlled the conversation effectively. Most importantly, we should ask what we could have done differently to create a better experience and review any note the customer left with the rating. Not every negative rating will necessarily be caused by something the agent did. Sometimes a customer may simply be unhappy with the outcome, policy, or situation.

However, even in those cases, reviewing the interaction can help us identify whether there was anything we could have communicated differently. A bad result does not automatically mean someone is a bad agent; one difficult interaction can provide a lesson that improves many future interactions. Accountability therefore means being willing to ask ourselves, "What can I learn from this, and what can I do better next time?" Don't just look at the rating; look for the lesson behind it.

Accountability also means being open when a manager, RTA, QA specialist, or another colleague approaches us with an idea about how we can improve. Feedback should not automatically be seen as criticism. When it is constructive and relevant, a professional approach is to listen, understand the reason behind it, ask questions when needed, and turn that feedback into practical action. Applying useful feedback can help us work more accurately, become more productive, strengthen our skills, and avoid repeating the same mistakes. The goal is not simply to receive feedback; it is to use it as a tool for continuous improvement.

9. Keep an Open Mind

Having an open mind is very important for learning and becoming a better customer-support specialist. We receive feedback from quality teams, Team Leads, managers, and sometimes other departments because the role requires us to communicate and work with different parts of the company. During 1:1 meetings with your Team Lead or other managers, it is important to have an open mind when receiving feedback so you can work on the areas that need improvement.

As agents, we face many customers throughout the day, and sometimes a third person who was not directly involved in the situation, such as QA or a Team Lead, can see something from a different perspective. Getting feedback from them can therefore be very helpful. When you have time, review your own performance. Look at where you can be better, where your strong and weak sides are, and what you can do to improve. You can even ask teammates who are strong in an area where you are weaker for some tips. This can work both ways - you can also share advice in areas where you are stronger.

This helps the team learn from each other while contributing to a friendly and professional working environment.

10. Use Tools Effectively and Be Creative

Customer-service tools exist to make our work easier, faster, and more consistent. Templates, internal knowledge resources, automated systems, shortcuts, and other tools can help us manage our workload, maintain response times, and give us more time to concentrate on the customer.

Use Templates as Your Best Friend

Templates can be one of the most useful tools in customer service, particularly during a busy day when we may be handling difficult conversations alongside more straightforward requests. A challenging interaction can consume a lot of mental energy, and without realizing it, we may carry that frustration into the next conversation. Templates help us maintain professional and consistent communication without recreating the same responses repeatedly, improving efficiency and allowing us to focus more attention on the customer's situation. However, a template should never replace human communication.

It is a starting point that should be adjusted to fit the customer's situation, communication style, and specific concern. Simply copying and pasting the same response regardless of what the customer has told us can damage the customer experience. A good template helps us work faster; a professional customer service specialist knows when and how to personalize it.

In Chat Support, Let the Customer Know You're Human

One of the biggest lessons I have learned specifically from chat-based customer service is the importance of making the human connection clear. When a customer enters a chat and immediately says, "I need a human agent," we should pay attention to what they are really asking for. Customers regularly interact with automated systems and chatbots, so if our response appears instantly or sounds heavily scripted, they may still wonder whether they are speaking with another automated system. Naturally letting the customer know that a real person is handling their case can immediately help build trust.

Once customers understand that a human agent is reading their messages, listening to their situation, and personally trying to help, they may become more willing to explain what happened and engage with the conversation. This can be particularly important when the customer is already frustrated or has struggled to get the help they need. Use technology for efficiency, but never allow efficiency to remove the human connection.

11. Understand Your KPIs

KPIs help measure different parts of customer-service performance. The exact measurements can vary from one company to another, but some common examples are CSAT/DSAT, FCR, AHT, and Quality.

CSAT/DSAT - Customer satisfaction and dissatisfaction can help show how customers experienced the support they received.

FCR - First Contact Resolution focuses on whether the customer's issue was resolved without unnecessary repeat contact.

AHT - Average Handling Time measures how long interactions take. Efficiency matters, but speed should not come at the expense of understanding the customer or providing the correct solution.

Quality

One KPI that should never be underestimated is quality. Quality is not only about following the correct process or giving the customer the correct answer; it also means making sure that the interaction is handled and recorded correctly from beginning to end. This includes understanding the customer's reason for contacting us, selecting the correct contact category or case classification, providing accurate information, following the correct procedure, documenting the interaction properly when required, and making sure the customer receives the right response and next steps.

Selecting the correct category may seem like a small administrative task, but it can have a much bigger impact on the company. When customer contacts are categorized accurately, the company can better understand why customers are contacting customer service, what the most frequent reasons for contact are, and where customers may be experiencing repeated problems or confusion. If a particular issue becomes one of the most common reasons for contact, accurate data can help the company ask important questions: Why are customers contacting us about this? Is something unclear in the process? Could we communicate something better?

Could the website, product, service, self-service option, or internal process be improved? This is why quality should not be viewed only as a score given to an individual agent. Information recorded during customer interactions can become valuable feedback for the wider business, but it is only useful when it is accurate. Selecting the wrong category, providing incorrect information, or failing to follow the correct process can affect both the individual customer interaction and the quality of the data the company uses to make decisions.

12. Final Tip: Take Care of Your Mental Well-Being

Customer service requires concentration, patience, empathy, and energy, which means our mental well-being directly affects how we communicate and perform our responsibilities. When we are mentally exhausted, distracted, or overwhelmed, it becomes easier to miss important information, lose patience, communicate poorly, or make unnecessary mistakes. Taking care of ourselves is therefore part of maintaining professional performance. Using our breaks properly and taking time to reset can help us maintain concentration and patience throughout the day.

Quieter periods can also be used productively to improve our knowledge, review previous interactions, improve templates, or organize resources that make future work easier. Taking care of our mental well-being is not about avoiding responsibility; it helps us remain in the right condition to perform our responsibilities properly.

Minimize Distractions at Work

Minimizing distractions is particularly important when we are new to a customer-service role because this is when we are building our foundation. We are learning the company's policies, products, systems, tools, processes, communication standards, and different customer scenarios at the same time. Strong concentration during this stage makes it easier to absorb that knowledge and build confidence.

During this period, focus on understanding how things work, learn from experienced colleagues, ask questions when something is unclear, review your own interactions, learn where to find information, and become comfortable with the tools you use every day. As your knowledge and confidence develop, many tasks will naturally become easier to manage. A strong foundation makes everything that comes afterward easier.

Create a Friendly Working Environment - It's Everyone's Responsibility

Creating a positive working environment is not the responsibility of one person, manager, or department; it is something every employee contributes to. Alongside our main responsibilities, the way we communicate, support each other, share knowledge, and behave toward our colleagues shapes the environment in which everyone works. Customer-service agents contribute by creating a respectful and welcoming atmosphere among colleagues. Managers contribute by managing performance while creating an environment where employees can communicate, learn, and perform effectively.

Trainers contribute by teaching while making people comfortable asking questions and developing their skills. Workforce-management teams, operations specialists, quality teams, support functions, and other departments all contribute in their own ways.

Quick Summary
Listen → Understand → Empathize → Confirm → Advise → Resolve → Verify → Close.

Listen — Give the customer the opportunity to explain their request and pay attention to the details.

Understand — Identify what the customer actually needs, including the reason behind the initial question.

Empathize — Acknowledge the customer’s situation naturally and appropriately.

Confirm — Paraphrase the request and make sure you and the customer have the same understanding.

Advise — Use your knowledge of the company, policies, products, and services to explain the solution and, where appropriate, the available alternatives. Give the customer enough information to make an informed decision.

Resolve — Complete the appropriate action or clearly guide the customer through the next steps.

Verify — Before closing, review the interaction and make sure every question or request has been addressed. In chat and email, go back through the previous messages to check whether anything was missed. The same principle applies to calls by mentally reviewing what was discussed before ending the conversation.

Close — Ask whether there is anything else you can help with before ending the interaction. This gives the customer one final opportunity to raise something that may have been missed and helps ensure the conversation ends with confidence.

A professional interaction should not end simply because the first question was answered. It should end when we are confident the customer’s needs have been addressed.

CHAPTER 1 — FINALIZING

Bringing It All Together

Ultimately, professional customer service comes from combining listening, understanding, communication, empathy, knowledge, confidence, efficiency, consistency, accountability, and an open mind. These qualities are connected.

Listening helps us understand; understanding allows us to communicate and empathize properly; knowledge gives us confidence; confidence helps us work efficiently; consistency supports our performance; accountability helps us recognize where we need to improve; and continuous improvement allows us to keep developing throughout our careers.

A professional customer service specialist is not someone who never makes mistakes. A professional customer service specialist is someone who learns from those mistakes, makes sure the same mistake becomes less likely to happen again, and turns mistakes into opportunities for positive change and lessons that improve future customer experiences.

CHAPTER 2

Becoming a Strong Team Leader or Team Manager

Being a Team Lead is not simply about giving instructions, helping agents when they have questions, checking their numbers, or making sure they reach their KPIs. A Team Lead has responsibility for the performance of the team, but also for supporting, guiding, developing, and protecting the people behind that performance while protecting the company and its standards.

1. Lead Beyond the KPIs

Team managers are there to help employees in every possible professional way. Hitting targets matters, but numbers alone do not explain what is happening inside a team. Strong leadership means understanding the employees, knowledge, systems, processes, and working environment behind those numbers.

2. Understand What Drives Employee Performance

When an employee's performance drops or they are consistently not reaching their KPIs, the first question should not always be, "Why aren't you hitting your target?" A better question can be, "What is preventing you from reaching it?" There may be a reason behind the numbers. Perhaps the employee needs more knowledge about the company's products or services, is struggling with a system, does not fully understand a procedure, or has difficulty finding the correct information. A good Team Lead should investigate before making assumptions.

If the problem is knowledge, identify the knowledge gap and help the employee improve it. If the problem is the system, help them become more comfortable using it. If they are struggling with a particular KPI, review their work together and identify which behaviors are affecting that KPI. The purpose should not only be to tell an employee what they are doing wrong. It should be to help them understand how they can do it better.

Recognize Potential and Create Opportunities

A Team Leader should know their employees beyond their KPI results. Understanding their strengths, skills, interests, consistency, and potential can help identify where they may be able to contribute beyond their current responsibilities.

When an employee consistently performs well, demonstrates dedication, or shows particular strengths, strong performance should not simply result in more work. Additional tasks can be valuable when they provide a genuine opportunity to learn, demonstrate new skills, and prepare for future responsibilities. However, a Team Lead should also make sure that an employee's hard work and dedication are recognized rather than repeatedly adding responsibilities without development or acknowledgment.

When an employee consistently performs at a high level and shows potential for growth, the Team Lead should consider raising this with Operations and discussing possible development opportunities, expanded responsibilities with appropriate recognition, or progression into another role. A good Team Lead should not only identify employees who need support; they should also identify employees who may be ready for their next opportunity.

Leadership is not only about improving performance; it is also about recognizing potential and helping employees turn strong performance into professional growth.

3. Keep Your Team Informed and Supported

From the moment employees start their shift until they finish, much of their attention is focused on customers: reading queries, handling chats, answering calls or emails, searching for information, solving problems, documenting interactions, and maintaining performance. While employees are concentrating on those responsibilities, the Team Lead should make sure they have the information and support necessary to do their jobs properly.

If HR communicates an important requirement, a new knowledge-base article is published, a procedure changes, or a product or service is updated, make sure that information reaches employees clearly and on time. We cannot expect an employee to follow an update they were never properly informed about.

4. Balance Employee Support With Company Standards

Supporting employees does not mean accepting everything they do. A Team Lead has responsibilities in both directions: supporting the employee while maintaining company standards. If an employee is doing something outside company policy or procedure, it needs to be addressed. Whenever possible, the purpose should not simply be punishment. Understand what happened, explain what needs to change, bring the employee back within company protocol, and help them improve.

Sometimes a mistake happens because an employee misunderstood a procedure, lacked knowledge, developed an incorrect working habit, or simply made the wrong decision. A strong Team Lead identifies the cause and works with the employee to correct it. At the same time, when something is serious, the Team Lead has a responsibility to take the appropriate action required by company policy. Supporting an employee and maintaining company standards are not opposites. Good leadership requires both.

5. Make One-to-One Meetings Meaningful

One-to-one meetings should not become meetings where the Team Lead simply opens a dashboard, reads an employee's numbers, tells them what is below target, and ends the conversation. A 1:1 is an opportunity to understand the employee behind those numbers. Ask how things are going, what they are struggling with, and whether something at work is preventing them from performing at their normal level.

Listening is important, but a Team Lead may sometimes be able to offer more than listening. Based on their own professional or life experience, they may have advice, another perspective, or a possible solution that could help. A manager cannot solve every personal problem, and that is not their responsibility, but they can still show genuine human understanding. This builds trust and can help problems be identified before they become more serious.

6. Resolve Employee Conflict Early and Fairly

Conflict between employees should not be ignored or allowed to grow. Small misunderstandings can quickly affect communication, teamwork, the working environment, and eventually performance if they are not addressed.

When a Team Lead becomes aware of a conflict, they should try to understand the situation as early as possible. Listen to the employees involved individually when appropriate, avoid making assumptions or immediately taking sides, and understand what actually happened before deciding how to approach the situation.

The goal should not simply be to decide who is right or wrong. The priority is to help the employees involved communicate professionally, understand each other's perspective, and find a way to move forward while maintaining respect and company standards. Not every disagreement requires a major intervention, but when conflict begins affecting teamwork, performance, communication, or the working environment, the Team Lead should take ownership rather than hoping the problem will resolve itself.

Resolving conflict early protects the employees involved and helps maintain the stability, professionalism, and performance of the entire team.

7. From Top Performer to Effective Team Leader

Being a top-performing employee and being a strong Team Lead are not the same responsibility. As an employee, you are primarily responsible for your own performance. When you become a Team Leader, that responsibility becomes much bigger because you are now responsible for helping an entire team perform.

The knowledge, habits, and experience that made you a top performer can be extremely valuable, but your goal is no longer simply to demonstrate how well you can perform. Your challenge is learning how to transfer that experience to employees and help them develop their own performance. Different employees will have different strengths, weaknesses, personalities, learning styles, and levels of experience. A strong Team Lead learns the team and adapts their leadership accordingly.

8. Take Ownership and Turn Frontline Insights Into Improvements

When you see something that needs to be done, do not always leave it for the next person. If something is making employees' jobs unnecessarily difficult, if a process is creating repeated problems, or if you identify a better way of doing something, address it when it is within your responsibility and raise it to the appropriate people when it is not.

Team Leaders work close to frontline employees and may notice problems that are not immediately visible to Operations or other departments. When meeting with Operations managers, share those observations. Whenever possible, do not only bring a problem: explain its impact and bring a possible solution. A Team Lead can become an important bridge between frontline employees and the wider operation, helping useful ideas move beyond the team and become improvements for employees, customers, and the company.

9. Build a Professional Workplace and Manage Conflict Effectively

Strong leadership includes creating a workplace where employees feel respected, supported, and comfortable contributing. Employees should not constantly feel, "I have to go to work." The aim should be to create an environment where employees can feel positive about coming to work, communicating with colleagues, asking for help, and contributing to the team.

Recognition matters. It should not belong only to the highest performer. An employee who has significantly improved, consistently helps colleagues, demonstrates reliability, shares knowledge, or contributes positively to the working environment may also deserve acknowledgment.

A professional workplace also requires leaders to pay attention to relationships within the team. When tension or conflict appears, it should be managed fairly and early so that it does not damage communication, employee morale, teamwork, or performance. Employees should feel respected regardless of nationality, culture, background, education, personality, or where they come from. Leadership sets the standard for how employees treat one another and how disagreements are handled.

10. Develop Employees, Maintain Standards, and Lead by Example

At the end of the day, Team Leaders have many responsibilities, and hitting targets is certainly one of them. But achieving the target should not be separated from developing the employees responsible for achieving it.

A strong Team Lead helps employees improve, identifies what is holding them back, keeps them informed, listens to them, coaches them, recognizes their strengths, addresses problems when necessary, maintains company standards, and creates opportunities for professional growth. Leadership also means demonstrating the same professionalism, accountability, communication, and respect that you expect from your employees.

Leading by example creates credibility. When employees see that their Team Lead takes ownership, handles conflict fairly, follows standards, communicates professionally, recognizes good work, and remains willing to learn, those behaviors become part of the team's culture.

Don't only manage the KPI. Develop the employees who create the KPI, recognize their potential, maintain the standards that guide them, and lead in a way they can learn from.

FINAL REFLECTION

Final Reflection

Customer service and leadership are connected by the same basic principle: people perform better when they understand what is expected of them, have the knowledge and support to do it, and are treated with professionalism and respect. For customer service specialists, professionalism means listening, learning, communicating clearly, taking accountability, understanding the company, and continuously improving. For Team Leads, professionalism means creating the conditions that allow those qualities to grow across an entire team. The experiences and opinions I have shared in this book come from my own professional journey.

They represent lessons I have learned through working with customers, colleagues, teams, challenges, mistakes, feedback, and continuous improvement. My experience may not be identical to yours, but I hope that some of these lessons can help you build your own approach and become stronger in your role. Strong customer service creates confidence in the company. Strong leadership creates the environment that makes strong customer service possible.

BEFORE YOU CLOSE THIS BOOK

Before You Close This Book

Whether you are starting your first customer service role, developing your experience, or taking responsibility for a team, remember that becoming better does not happen in one day. Listen. Learn. Ask questions. Accept feedback. Take responsibility for your mistakes. Share what you know. Support the people around you. And never stop looking for ways to improve. You do not need to know everything to be a professional. You need to be willing to keep learning. Every customer can teach you something. Every mistake can teach you something. Every colleague can teach you something.

And every day is another opportunity to become better than you were yesterday. Thank you for reading.
